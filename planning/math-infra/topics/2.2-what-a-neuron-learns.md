# M · What a neuron learns — build blueprint

**Slug:** `math-infra/what-a-neuron-learns` · **Level:** 200 · **Difficulty:** intermediate · **~12 min**
**Prerequisites:** `math-infra/gradient-descent` · **relatedTo:** `math-infra/what-is-a-tensor`, `ml-systems/why-tpus`
**Path:** `math-infra` (L200 · Math of Learning — **after gradient-descent**) · **Audience:** infra/SRE ("Sam"), no calculus.

## TLDR — why / what / how
**Why.** Gradient descent taught the *loop* abstractly. But "the model" is still a black box. This makes
it concrete: the smallest real model — a **single neuron** — actually learns a decision rule from data,
via *real* gradient descent. Once an operator sees that "a model is just a pile of weights you nudge,"
checkpoints, HBM footprint, and inference-vs-training stop being mysterious.
**What.** A neuron = weighted sum of inputs + bias, squashed by an **activation** (sigmoid) into a 0–1
score. Training nudges the **weights** to minimize loss (real GD). What it learns is a **decision
boundary**. Inference is one forward pass. Scale this neuron to billions and you have an LLM.
**How.** One sim, `neuron-explorer` (reworked from the user's React prototype): a live neuron diagram +
optimizer feedback loop, a decision-boundary graph over a tiny relatable dataset (cars: price + color →
buy/walk), real Step / Auto-Train / Reset, live weight bars, and an inference panel.

## Accuracy gates (verify at draft)
- **Neuron:** output = activation(Σ wᵢxᵢ + b). Inputs normalized to 0–1. Sigmoid squashes to a 0–1
  score; **decision boundary at score 0.5** is a straight line in input space (linear classifier).
- **Real gradient descent:** weights updated by `w -= lr * gradient`; gradient ≈ `error × input`
  averaged over the dataset; LR 0.5; early-stop at loss < 0.01. (Note: `error×input` omits the sigmoid
  derivative — it's the cross-entropy gradient, not MSE's; converges fine, invisible at this level. Keep,
  but don't claim it's "the MSE gradient.")
- **Classification vs regression:** the gradient-descent topic fit a *line to numbers* (regression);
  this *separates two classes* (classification). Call out the contrast.
- **Weights = the model.** The learned w₁, w₂, b ARE the model's state — what you checkpoint and what
  fills HBM at scale. This is the operational anchor.
- A single neuron can only learn a **straight** boundary; real nets stack many to bend it. One honest line.

## Learning objectives
1. State what a neuron computes (weighted sum + bias → activation → score).
2. Explain that "the model" is just its **weights/parameters**, and training nudges them.
3. Read a **decision boundary** as "the rule the model learned."
4. Distinguish **training** (the expensive loop that sets weights) from **inference** (one forward pass).

## Must-learn spine (SRE bridge per beat)
1. **A neuron is a weighted vote.** Each input times a weight, summed, plus a bias, squashed to a score.
   *SRE bridge:* like a weighted health-check score deciding in/out of rotation — except the weights are
   *learned*, not hand-tuned.
2. **The weights ARE the model.** Training = nudging w₁, w₂, b downhill on the loss (real GD here).
   *SRE bridge:* the weights are the stateful artifact you **checkpoint** and that **eats HBM** — billions
   of these numbers in an LLM. ([[what-is-a-tensor]] — they're just a big tensor.)
3. **What it learns is a boundary/rule.** Watch the purple line rotate into place separating buy/walk.
   *SRE bridge:* the model is a learned decision rule, not hand-written `if` statements.
4. **Train once, infer forever.** Training is the slow loop; inference is a single cheap forward pass.
   *SRE bridge:* the cost asymmetry behind separate training vs serving fleets — you pay big once, then
   serve cheap (and at scale, serving still dominates total compute).
