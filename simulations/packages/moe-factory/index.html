Why Modern LLMs are Switching to MoE

Historically, large language models were "dense" — every parameter of the model was activated to process every token. An 80-billion parameter model runs 80 billion calculations for the word "the", and another 80 billion for "cat". This scales linearly, resulting in massive hosting costs and astronomical training latency.

Mixture of Experts (MoE) is a structural paradigm that transforms a large model into an array of smaller, specialized subnetworks (the "experts"). Instead of executing the entire model, a small neural gate (the Gating Network) reads the input token and instantly computes routing probabilities. Only the top $k$ experts are awakened to handle the token — Sparse Activation.

Scale without OverheadAn MoE model can have 600 billion parameters total, but only use 12 billion parameters per token — the accuracy of a huge model at the cost of a small one.
FFN SpecializationThrough joint backpropagation, individual Feed-Forward Networks (FFNs) discover niches — syntax, math, vision, or specific languages.
The Gating NetworkA small, parametric linear gate trained alongside the experts, so routing matches the strengths of downstream subnetworks.

The Mathematical Foundation of Sparse Routing

Let an MoE layer consist of $N$ Feed-Forward Networks $E_1, E_2, \dots, E_N$. Given an input token embedding $x$, the aggregated output $y$ is:

$$y = \sum_{i=1}^{N} G(x)_i E_i(x)$$

where $G(x)_i$ is the routing score for the $i$-th expert. To enforce sparsity, the raw logits pass through a $\text{KeepTop-}k$ that zeroes all but the top $k$ values ($k \in \{1, 2\}$):

$$G(x) = \text{Softmax}\left(\text{KeepTop-}k\left(W_g \cdot x + \epsilon, k\right)\right)$$

$W_g$ is the learned routing weight matrix, and $\epsilon$ is low-amplitude noise injected during training to balance load and stop one "popular" expert from starving the others of gradient.
