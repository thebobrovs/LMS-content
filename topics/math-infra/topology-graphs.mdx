## The one-line summary

The previous lesson left the gradient all-reduce riding "the interconnect" — but what *is* that?
**Just a graph.** Devices are **nodes**, links are **edges**, and the speed of a collective on it comes
down to two graph numbers you already reason about under other names: the **<Term id="network diameter">diameter</Term>** (the worst-case hop count — your latency bound) and the **<Term id="bisection bandwidth">bisection</Term>** (the links you'd cut to split it in half — your cross-section throughput).
Change the *shape* of the graph and those numbers move — which is why two clusters with the **same number
of devices** can train at very different speeds.

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** You've drawn this graph a hundred times. A **spine-leaf / Clos** fabric is a graph; so
is a **ring**, a **mesh**, a **<Term id="torus">torus</Term>**. The two questions you ask of any of them
— *what's the farthest hop?* (tail latency) and *what's the throughput if traffic crosses the middle?*
(spine oversubscription) — are exactly diameter and bisection. This topic just hands you the vocabulary
map.
</Callout>

## Two metrics that cap a collective

- **<Term id="network diameter">Network diameter</Term>** — the longest of all the shortest paths: the
  most <Term id="hop count">hops</Term> any message ever needs. It bounds worst-case **latency**.
- **<Term id="bisection bandwidth">Bisection bandwidth</Term>** — slice the network into two equal halves;
  the number of links you must cut, times their bandwidth, is the **throughput across the middle**. It
  bounds **all-reduce / all-to-all** (the pattern where every node exchanges with every other) traffic,
  which by definition crosses the whole network.

## Shape is the lever — same devices, faster network

Two moves, both visible in the sim, change the metrics without adding a single device:

- **Wrap-around (mesh → torus).** Closing each row and column into a ring gives every node a second way
  around, so the worst-case path — the **diameter** — roughly **halves**. (It also doubles the bisection,
  since now there are two cut points across each ring, not one.)
- **Square up the grid.** A long, thin `4×16` mesh has a tiny bisection (few links span its narrow waist)
  and a big diameter (far corners). A square `8×8` of the same 64 nodes has a **wider bisection** and a
  **shorter diameter**. Shape, for free.

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** This is the same instinct as **not stringing your fabric out in a long chain**: keep it
compact and richly cross-linked so no two endpoints are far apart and the middle isn't a chokepoint. A
torus is "the ring trick, in every dimension."
</Callout>

## Fat-tree vs torus — the cost/throughput call

The shape most large GPU and datacenter clusters use is the **<Term id="fat-tree">fat-tree</Term>** (a
Clos / spine-leaf): a switched tree engineered for **full, non-blocking bisection** — any node can talk to
any node at line rate. The price is **more switches and links**. A **torus** is the other end: cheap
(every node is just wired to its neighbors, no big switch tier) but **blocking** — its bisection is
limited, so the biggest collectives can congest it. Same trade you already make choosing a non-blocking
spine vs an oversubscribed one: **throughput vs cost.**

## It's the all-reduce's speed limit

Tie it back: the gradient **all-reduce** from the last lesson is a workload that hammers the *whole*
network every step. So its time is governed by exactly these two metrics — diameter (how long the
slowest leg takes) and bisection (whether the cross-network traffic congests). That's why, past raw
bandwidth, **two levers** matter most:

- **Cluster shape** — a well-connected topology (fat-tree, or a squarer/wrapped torus) finishes
  collectives faster.
- **Job placement** — packing a job onto a *compact, well-connected sub-graph* (rather than scattering it
  across a sparse slice) keeps its diameter small and its links off the congested middle. The knob is
  **topology-aware placement** in the scheduler — an affinity rule or placement group that keeps the job
  on adjacent, well-connected nodes instead of wherever capacity happens to be free.

## Why this matters

- **You can read any fabric the same way.** Ring, torus, fat-tree, spine-leaf — all graphs; diameter and
  bisection tell you the latency and throughput story without vendor specifics.
- **Topology is a first-class capacity decision**, not an afterthought — it sets the ceiling on how well
  your gradient sync (and thus your training throughput) scales.
- **Placement is an operator lever.** "Why is this run slow?" can be "it got scattered across a
  high-diameter slice," and the fix is packing it tighter — no new hardware.
