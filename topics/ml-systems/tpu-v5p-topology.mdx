## The one-line summary

A TPU v5p pod is **8,960 chips wired into a 3D torus**. Each chip talks to six
neighbours over **ICI** (Inter-Chip Interconnect); the grid wraps around at its
edges so there are no "ends," which keeps any two chips a short hop apart. You
rent a **slice** of that torus and choose its shape to match how you split your
model.

## The hierarchy: chip → host → cube → slice → pod → Multislice

Scaling on a TPU isn't one jump from "a chip" to "a supercomputer" — it's a
ladder, and each rung is bound by a different tier of network. Step through it:

<Simulation id="scale-hierarchy" height={460} />

The thing to notice: every rung up to a **pod** is still one ICI torus. Only
<Term>Multislice</Term> crosses pods, and that hop runs over the slower{" "}
<Term id="dcn">DCN</Term> — which is why where a boundary falls matters so much
for performance.

## The 3D torus, up close

Inside a slice, each chip has six <Term id="ici">ICI</Term> links — `±X`, `±Y`,
`±Z`. At the edges the
links **wrap around** to the opposite face, turning a 4×4×4 grid into a torus
with no boundary. That wrap is what keeps the network *diameter* (worst-case hop
count) low even as the slice grows.

Click a chip to light up its six neighbours, and toggle the wrap-around links to
watch the edge chips close the loop:

<Simulation id="torus-3d" height={560} />

<Callout type="note">
Low diameter is the whole point. On a ring of N chips the farthest message
travels N/2 hops; wrap a grid into a torus in 3D and the worst case grows like
the **cube root** of the chip count instead. That's why collective operations
(all-reduce, all-gather) stay fast at pod scale.
</Callout>

## Slice shape is a real lever

Two slices with the *same chip count* can train at very different speeds. A
`4×4×32` slice has one long axis — great for a **ring all-reduce** across a
data-parallel group. An `8×8×8` slice is balanced — better when you need high
bandwidth in every direction for **tensor parallelism**. Picking the shape is
part of designing the run, not an afterthought.

## Why this matters for ML

The three ways to split a model map onto the torus axes:

- <Term id="data parallelism">Data parallelism (DP)</Term> replicates the model
  and all-reduces gradients — it rides a torus ring happily, so it can stretch
  across a long axis.
- <Term id="tensor parallelism">Tensor parallelism (TP)</Term> splits a single
  matmul and is bandwidth-hungry, so it wants the tightest, highest-bandwidth ICI
  axis.
- <Term id="pipeline parallelism">Pipeline parallelism (PP)</Term> splits layers
  into stages; its traffic is smaller and more tolerant, so it can span looser
  links — even across a Multislice DCN boundary in large runs.

Get the mapping right and the interconnect disappears into the background. Get it
wrong — e.g. tensor-parallel traffic forced across the DCN — and the chips sit
idle waiting on the network.

## Inspect it in code

On a real `v5p-64` slice, JAX reports all 64 chips and their coordinates in the
ICI mesh — the same 4×4×4 torus the simulation draws:

```python title="inspect_topology.py"
import jax

devices = jax.devices()
print(len(devices), "TPU chips")          # 64 on a v5p-64 slice

# Each device knows its (x, y, z) coordinate in the 3D-torus mesh.
for d in sorted(devices, key=lambda d: d.coords)[:4]:
    print(d.id, d.coords, "core", d.core_on_chip)
```

The `coords` are exactly the axes you clicked through above — wrap-around and all.
